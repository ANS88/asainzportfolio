This week landed heavily on prediction models, which is a pattern worth naming: we have ten studies building or validating machine learning systems across maternal outcomes, fertility, and postpartum mental health.

The strongest thread is early warning systems that actually move the needle on timing, cardiotocography models catching fetal hypoxia three to six hours earlier than standard care, and time-aware approaches outperforming last-minute alerts. That's actionable. But here's what I'm watching: four preprints, and none of them peer-reviewed yet, which means we should hold tight on claims until those pass review. More important, nearly everything assumes you have clean data and access to the technology. The lactation study and the piece on PMOS treatment gaps in rural India both name what we don't have: lactation status isn't even recorded in administrative data, and whole regions lack access to basic care. Before we celebrate the next algorithm, I want to know who gets left out when it deploys. I'd suggest leading with the early warning timing story, it's concrete, and holding a second post on the evidence gaps the models expose.

This week I found two solid design-centered studies, both working the way I think research should. The first is a protocol for translating and testing a Spanish contraception decision tool, doing the hard iterative work of cultural adaptation alongside usability testing rather than bolting translation on at the end. The second is a cognitive interview study refining a questionnaire for endometriosis by actually listening to how patients experience and describe their symptoms, which means the tool will measure what matters instead of what researchers assumed mattered. Both are still in pilot phases, so the real test comes when they move to implementation, but the methodology itself is the kind that catches the gaps early.
