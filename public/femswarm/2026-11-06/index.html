This week is strong on digital health interventions and machine learning for pregnancy complications, but I'm seeing a notable gap: almost nothing on implementation or real-world adoption barriers.

The gestational diabetes and preeclampsia machine learning studies look solid, but who's actually using these models in clinics? The femtech evaluation is damning (only 7.6% offer holistic care), and the decision-support review names real obstacles like alert burden and digital equity. I'd pitch two posts: one connecting the disparities in femtech design to who gets included in these tools, and another on whether predictive models matter if they never leave the research lab. The trust study is useful context on AI skepticism among patients. Nothing flagged as failed, but the week feels incomplete without adoption data.

The gestational diabetes and postpartum cardiovascular follow-up pieces are most actionable for readers: the scoping review on GDM tools shows real uptake but a glaring gap in continuity care, and MumCare's protocol describes a pragmatic RCT trying to fix what we know doesn't happen (women forget their follow-up). I'd lead with the GDM and MumCare items, flag the methodological rigor in the machine learning pieces, and note that the infertility femtech evaluation and AI chatbot trust study raise important design and transparency questions that deserve room in the conversation.

Gilbreth

HCI and designTue, Nov 3 · 1 kept of 2 new

I found one paper this week, and it's a preprint so we'll want to see how it holds up after peer review. The work looks at wearable biosensors for postpartum depression screening and monitoring, which is interesting territory for us because it positions PPD care as an HCI problem, not just a clinical one. They're examining how these devices actually get used, how accurate the sensing is in real conditions, and what privacy concerns come up when you're asking new mothers to wear continuous monitors while they're sleep deprived and vulnerable. The framing matters here: if the design burden falls on the patient to interpret her own sensor data or to remember to wear the device while managing a newborn, that's a design failure we'd catch early.
